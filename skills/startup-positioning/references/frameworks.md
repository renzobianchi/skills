# Frameworks

The procedures behind phases 2 to 4, as Fletch names them. Each is inputs → steps → output.

## Minimum Viable Positioning (MVP) canvas

Inputs: what is known about market and product. Steps: identify champions → identify company types → identify contextual anchors (use cases, broken down as small as the product supports) → identify competitive anchors (the current way) → pick one option and craft the argument as a ten-second elevator pitch. Rule: ONE element from the market side and ONE from the product side (at most two each). Output: `<category or use case> for <target customer> that <differentiation>`.

The 2025 revision adds a **differentiation summary** ("why the current way sucks, how you do it differently, why that is better") and a **positioning strategy summary** that says whether the strategy is category-based or use-case-based. Either the category box or the use-case box must be filled.

## Market maturity → positioning type

Diagnose first; the anchor follows.

| Stage | Signal | Positioning type | Elements |
|---|---|---|---|
| Immature | The activity is new; nobody does it yet | Outcome-based | unmet desired outcome, the activity you propose, alternative activities, differentiation |
| Emerging | The activity exists, done with bad general tools | Use-case-based | the activity, your category of tool, alternative categories, differentiation |
| Mature | Most of the market solves it with the same category | Category-based | the category, your product, alternative products, differentiation |

Test: "If most people in your target market solve the same specific problem with the same specific product category, that category is very mature." Stage-3 companies fail by leading with outcomes; stage-2 companies fail by competing with unknown startups instead of the status quo. Differentiation follows the same arc: contextual → competitive → brand.

## Positioning anchors

"Positioning is simply choosing a frame of reference." Anchors: tool (the category or the alternative), use case, desired outcome (only when there is no known use case and no comparable tool). Extended set: target customer, problem, capability, feature, benefit, product category; pick one to three, lead with the clearest, which nine times out of ten is the main capability.

- **Competitive positioning** anchors on the current way and answers "what do you replace?"
- **Contextual positioning** anchors on the workflow and answers "when would I use you?"

Choose whichever makes the value clearer in less time.

## JTBD positioning map

Most B2B markets look like this: a few in-category vendors, and a much larger mass of DIY, legacy tools bent to the job, manual processes and agencies. Map every alternative for the job, then choose the one you PRIMARILY position against; differentiation is different against each one, and combining all the arguments produces a generic message.

## ICP scorecard

Prerequisite: segments defined by workflow (what they do, how they do it today, what problem), never by firmographics alone. Score each on problem severity, differentiation, access, and size (last, and only as "supports the next revenue goal"). Output: one strategic bet. Wedges: shrink the market (market wedge), shrink to one activity (product wedge), or both (product-market wedge, "almost guaranteed to work").

## Levels of specificity

TAM positioning (broad, needs a distribution advantage), SAM-level (explainable to several segments, room to grow), SOM-level (targeted programs, expensive; double down only once proven). Position for the next revenue milestone, not the pitch deck: Shopify went snowboard stores → sporting goods → SMB ecommerce.

## Determining the category

1. "We're so much more than a ___": the blank is the category.
2. When two categories are plausible, test three messages at once in prospect calls: each nearest category with a differentiating modifier, plus a cross-over ("Sheets, Zapier and a Notion database"); converge on what prospects repeat back.
3. Two kinds of category creation: radical innovation anchors on the use case and lets the category form; sub-category creation ("cloud CRM", "luxury EV") anchors on the existing category with a modifier.

## Positioning the problem

Problems are contextual: each anchor yields a different problem set. From meta-problem ("data is disconnected") to struggling moment (stakeholder feedback in emails cross-referenced against the design file). A problem worth positioning on has an owner, a budget line, and is the reason demos get booked. The anti-value prop (anti-persona, anti-problem, anti-solution, anti-benefit) says who is excluded.

## Positioning Strategy Visualizer

Per candidate: anchors, unique value (problem tied to one anchor + the differentiated solution), a sample hero color-labeled by element, thesis ("why SHOULD you make this bet"), risks ("why SHOULDN'T you"). Build four or five, lay them side by side, choose, put it on the homepage.

## Three ways founders arrive at positioning

Customer-focused (audience → workflows → alternatives → arguments; usually strongest), technology-focused (unique capability → weaker alternatives → who uses them), competition-focused (products replaced → audiences who hate them most). Use the founder's natural entry, then complete the other two legs.
