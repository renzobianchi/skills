# Rules for hero and section headlines

Grade every line against these. Each names the failure and a before/after from the source posts. Cite the number in an audit.

1. **Lead with the capability, close with the benefit.** Leading with the outcome forces the reader to dig for what the product does. Walnut leads with an outcome; Tango leads with what you do.
2. **Lead with what the product does; the business outcome closes.** Any vendor can claim the outcome. Stripe: "Financial infrastructure to grow your revenue" → "Accept payments, offer financial services and build custom revenue models."
3. **First-order benefits only.** The further from what the product does, the less believable. "More leads, less effort" → PhantomBuster "gets you contact information." Vidyard's "close more deals" is fourth order.
4. **The hero answers one of two questions:** "which of my tools does this replace?" or "which tasks in my job would this help me with?"
5. **The hero answers what is it, who is it for, what does it let me do.** FigJam, Mural and Miro heroes read like the first slide of an investor deck.
6. **Use the category the customer already understands.** Chipotle sells meat, not protein; the category you want to own later goes nowhere near the hero.
7. **Name a specific audience.** "Businesses" is the whole of B2B.
8. **Anchor on one use case.** Platform messaging shrinks in resonance with every added job. Airtable: "Connect everything. Achieve anything." → a line that says what, does, and for whom.
9. **New category → anchor to the use case,** or the product message is not understood (DataCebo).
10. **Write for the ignorant champion.** The executive never reads the homepage; the champion has the problem and no context.
11. **The homepage is the How page.** Vision and strategic narrative go on their own page (Tome).
12. **Clear over clever; a longer headline is fine.** The hero header is the only line guaranteed to be read. Fletch's own H1: "Let's fix your confusing positioning."
13. **Remove keynote language.** "Empowering next-gen teams to drive transformation" → "We help HR teams roll out benefits faster, with fewer errors."
14. **Stack elements; a three-word tagline obscures the product.** Stop stacking before the reader bounces.
15. **Element counts on the page:** personas 1 to 2, problems 1 to 3, category exactly 1, capabilities 1 to 3, features 1 to 5, benefits 1 to 3.
16. **Show real product UI in the hero.** No product image is a curse-of-knowledge symptom (Deasie).
17. **Saturated market → differentiating capabilities in the hero.** Arc's readers did not learn how it differed until onboarding.
18. **Problem lines use struggling moments, not root causes.** "Your data is siloed" → "I've read the meeting notes in our CRM, and I still have no idea what's happening with the deal" (Gong).
19. **Every capability names its feature.** "Operationalize your critical data" is not a capability.
20. **Plain feature names.** A branded feature is a word the reader has to learn before it means anything.
21. **Features only inside a use case and a problem.** Outcome-only and feature-only messaging are the two least customer-centric shapes.
22. **Multi-audience: one persona for one to three scrolls (Uber), not a channel-changer (Rippling).** Multi-product: prioritize > itemize > summarize.
23. **Results or ROI section, if forced, at the bottom.**
24. **Website = demand capture (capabilities); pre-website channels = demand creation (problems).**
25. **Clarity test before A/B test.** Ask a stranger "what do you think we do, and who is it for?"
26. **Differentiation above the fold.** Around buried its no-feedback feature four scrolls down. If it is the thing nobody else has, it is in the hero.
27. **Explain before the interactive demo.** Visitors need "what is it" before they will click into one.
